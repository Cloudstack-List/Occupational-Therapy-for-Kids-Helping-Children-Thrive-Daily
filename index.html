When Should a Child See an Occupational Therapist?

Some red flags that suggest a child may benefit from therapy:

  • Trouble with everyday tasks: buttoning, tying shoes, using utensils
  • Clumsiness, frequent tripping or falling
  • Strong sensory reactions (noise, textures, touch)
  • Difficulty concentrating or regulating emotions
  • Delays in developmental milestones (crawling, grasping)

If any of these persist, a screening by an occupational therapist can help determine whether a structured intervention is needed.

  • Enhancing Learning and School Participation – Occupational therapy integrates practical strategies that support classroom performance, attention, and interaction with peers.
  • Home Practice and Carryover – Therapists provide exercises and activities for home to reinforce skills learned in sessions, ensuring progress extends beyond the clinic.

This type of therapy helps children build the skills needed to succeed in daily life. It promotes independence, confidence, and practical abilities, supporting growth at home, in school, and within the community.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What does an occupational therapist do with children?
They assess and support development of motor, sensory, cognitive, self-care, and play skills. Therapy is delivered through meaningful tasks and age-appropriate activities.

2. When should a child see an occupational therapist?
When ongoing difficulties (6+ months) in coordination, daily living tasks, sensory over/under-responses, or school performance appear.

3. How long does occupational therapy take for kids?
Duration varies based on the child’s needs and goals. Typical programs last 8–16 weeks, with reviews every 4–6 weeks.

4. How is occupational therapy for kids done at home?
Therapists provide simple, guided tasks or play exercises. Parents receive videos or handouts to help carry over progress between sessions.

5. What are signs a child needs occupational therapy?
Look for persistent difficulties with fine motor tasks, self-care, sensory overwhelm, poor coordination, or delays in developmental milestones.

6. How do I choose the right occupational therapist for my child?
Check for credentials (AHPRA registration in Australia), pediatric experience, good rapport with your child, ability to provide measurable outcomes, and local accessibility.
